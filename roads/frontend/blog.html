From 100 teams to the top 5: building Nagrik Roads

We built a phone app that finds potholes, counts each one once and tells the city which to fix first. Here is what we built, how far we got, and what happened at the finish.

Team NagrikJain College of Engineering and Research, BelagaviNovember 2026

The problem

Every rider in an Indian city knows the pothole that has been there for months. People complain, but complaints get lost, the same pothole is reported fifty times, and nobody can tell which one is most dangerous. The HackValley 2K26 problem statement (PS 02) asked for an AI system that detects road damage and potholes. We wanted to go further and build something a city could actually use to get roads fixed.

What we built

Nagrik Roads turns any phone into a road-survey dashcam. You record a ride or upload photos and videos, and the system does the rest.

  • A lightweight YOLO26n model, trained on Indian roads, detects potholes and three kinds of cracks.
  • A frame-linking tracker follows each defect across video frames, so it is counted once.
  • GPS and image matching recognise a pothole that was already reported and update it instead of creating a duplicate.
  • Every defect gets a severity rating and an explainable 0–100 risk score based on road type, speed limit, nearby schools and hospitals, repeat reports and the rain forecast.
  • Officials can confirm, reject or correct each detection, and re-inspections show whether a defect is getting worse or has been repaired.
  • Repairs that fail within 90 days are flagged, and officers get one daily digest instead of hundreds of alerts.
  • Faces and number plates are blurred automatically, and it all runs on a laptop CPU with free, open tools.

Built on Nagrik

Nagrik Roads is part of Nagrik, our civic-accountability platform. Nagrik tracks wards, officers and public works across Indian cities. Through it, every pothole is linked to its BBMP ward, the officer responsible for it and recent road tenders: who was paid to fix that road, and how much. We merged both into one application, so the same site serves Nagrik and Nagrik Roads.

What happened at the final

We did not win. In our view, the awards were not decided fairly. We felt the final evaluation was biased and shaped by internal politics, not by the work the teams put on the table. We reached the final five on the strength of a working, deployed product, and we believe it was not judged on its merits at the end.

A result decided by politics is not a verdict on the product.

We are disappointed, but not discouraged. The app is still live, the code is public, and anyone can judge it for themselves.

What we learned

  • Build for the people who will use it, not only for the judges. Their verdict is the one that matters.
  • Real-world data beats benchmarks. Our tracker only started counting potholes correctly after we tested it on our own bike rides.
  • A detection is only useful if it reaches the person who can fix it. Linking defects to wards and officers was the part judges asked most about.
